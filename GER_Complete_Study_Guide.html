Foundations of Entrepreneurial Finance

Before you can raise money, value a startup, or structure a deal — you need to understand what a startup is, who the key players are, and why finance matters at every stage of a company's life.

1.1 The Three Key Parties in Every Enterprise

Every startup involves three essential groups of people. Understanding their roles is the foundation of everything else in this course.

PartyWho They AreWhat They WantWhat They Contribute
The Startup (Enterprise)The company being builtGrowth, survival, market shareThe product or service and its value to the world
The FoundersPeople who start and run the companyControl, wealth creation, vision fulfillmentIdeas, energy, sweat equity, leadership
Capital Providers (Investors)People or institutions who give moneyReturns on their investmentMoney (and sometimes expertise, networks)
Think of it like building a house. The founder is the architect who designs and oversees everything. The investor is the bank financing the construction. The startup is the house itself — the product of their combined effort.

1.2 Why People Start Businesses (Primary Motivators)

Not every business is started for the same reason. Founders have four primary motivators:

MotivatorWhat It MeansExample
Social ImpactSolving a societal problem, not just making moneyBuilding affordable healthcare tech for rural India
Profit GenerationMaximizing financial returnsStarting a SaaS company with 70%+ margins
LivelihoodEarning enough to live comfortablyA freelancer forming a consultancy company
Self-ActualizationFulfilling one's creative or intellectual potentialA researcher commercializing their scientific discovery

Returns to Capital Providers

Investors give money, but what do they get back? There are two categories:

  • Risk-Adjusted Returns: Maximizing financial gains relative to the level of risk taken. A higher-risk investment should give higher returns — otherwise, why take the risk?
  • Capital Preservation: A conservative goal where the business only aims to generate enough cash to repay the original amount invested. Think of it as "just give me my money back."
Capital preservation is typically the floor expectation. Most investors want returns above capital preservation — they want growth on top of getting their money back.

1.2.1 The Finance Function of an Enterprise

Why does a company need a finance department? It serves five essential purposes:

  1. Ensuring enterprise sustainability — Making sure the company doesn't run out of money
  2. Optimizing returns for capital providers — Making the money work as hard as possible
  3. Establishing performance metrics — KPIs, financial ratios, benchmarks
  4. Systematic disclosure of financial results — Transparent reporting to shareholders
  5. Designing incentive mechanisms — Pay structures that align employee behavior with company goals (e.g., ESOPs)

1.3 The Phased Enterprise Lifecycle

A startup doesn't grow uniformly. It passes through distinct phases, each with different challenges and needs:

PHASE 1 (0–12 months) → PHASE 2 (13–36 months) → PHASE 3 (36–60 months) │ │ │ Firming up Early commercialization Commercial expansion manufacturing Product-Market Fit New product lines Limited pre- Testing sales & Full go-to-market commercial sales distribution options strategy
Phase 1 is like a baby learning to walk. Phase 2 is a child learning to run. Phase 3 is an adult sprinting in a race — you know where you're going and you're pushing hard.

Advantages of Early-Stage Innovative Startups

  • Agile Decision Making: Small teams can pivot quickly without bureaucracy
  • Scalable Growth Potential: Technology-driven companies can grow exponentially without proportional cost increases
  • Best-Practice Integration: Can adopt the latest tools and methods from day one
  • Novel Operating Model: Not bound by legacy systems or processes
  • High Innovation Index: Free to experiment, fail, and learn quickly

Vulnerabilities of Early-Stage Startups

  • Liability of Smallness: Small companies get worse terms from suppliers, banks, and customers
  • Lack of Benefit of Age: No track record means no trust; no trust means no credit
  • Liability of Novelty: New products face skepticism — customers are reluctant to try unproven things
  • Resource Acquisition Constraints: Hard to attract top talent without paying big salaries you can't afford yet

1.5 Types of Financing Instruments

Debt Financing

Debt means borrowing money. When a startup takes debt, it promises to:

  1. Pay periodic interest (like rent for the money)
  2. Repay the principal (the original amount borrowed) at the end

Other fees may also apply: processing fee, upfront fee, prepayment penalty.

When Borrower Defaults (Can't Pay)Lender's Response
Step 1Soft reminders — phone calls, emails
Step 2Legal notice — formal written warning
Step 3Court intervention — legal proceedings
Step 4Establishing insolvency — company is declared unable to pay debts
Debt is generally NOT suitable for early-stage startups because startups have uncertain cash flows. If you can't make your interest payment, you could lose everything — including your personal assets if you gave personal guarantees.

Equity Financing

Equity means selling a piece of ownership in your company in exchange for money. The investor becomes a part-owner (shareholder).

Key features of equity:

  • Balanced Risk and Reward: If the company fails, the investor loses money too. If it succeeds, both win.
  • Patient Capital: Equity investors are willing to wait years for their return (unlike debt which demands regular payments)
  • Permanent Capital: Equity stays on the company's books indefinitely unless bought back through a share buyback
  • Dividends: The surplus profit after tax distributed to equity shareholders. This is a discretionary payment — the board decides whether to pay dividends or reinvest the money
  • Capital Appreciation: Increase in the value (price) of shares over time
Debt is like renting a car — you pay every month no matter what, and if you can't pay, the car gets taken away. Equity is like having a business partner — if the business does well, you both share the profits; if it fails, you both share the loss.

Hybrid Financing Instrument

Combines characteristics of both debt AND equity. The most common example is Preference Share Capital:

  • Has a fixed rate of return (like debt)
  • Has predictable cash flow (like a loan)
  • But is technically ownership in the company (like equity)

Key Considerations in Equity Financing

  1. Distribution of Ownership (Cash Flow Rights): Shareholders have the right to a share of profits and assets
  2. Management Control & Voting Rights: 1 share = 1 vote. Shareholders vote on major decisions. Giving away equity means giving away some control.

1.7 Sources of Equity Capital

STARTUP FUNDING LADDER Stage 1: Idea / Pre-revenue ├── Three Fs: Founders, Family & Friends ├── Developmental Agencies (Grants) └── Incubators & Accelerators Stage 2: MVP / Early Traction ├── Angel Investors (individuals) └── Angel Networks (groups of angels) Stage 3: Product-Market Fit ├── Venture Capital (VC) └── Private Equity (PE) — later stage Stage 4: Scale / Pre-IPO └── Public Equity Market (Stock Exchange)

Three Fs — Founders, Family, and Friends

The very first source of capital for almost every startup. These people invest based on personal trust in the founder, not business analysis.

Developmental Agencies

Government agencies found in countries where financial markets are underdeveloped. Their goal is to help startups grow, not to earn profit. Examples include government grants, SIDBI in India.

Incubators and Accelerators

  • Incubators: Provide infrastructure, mentorship, and resources to early-stage startups to develop their idea. Usually longer programs (1-3 years)
  • Accelerators: Intensive, short-term programs (3-6 months) that help startups grow rapidly. Famous examples: Y Combinator, 500 Global

Angel Investors

Angel investors are high net worth individuals — such as former entrepreneurs, executives, or wealth inheritors — who invest their personal surplus savings into early-stage ventures. They are called "angels" because they come to a startup's rescue when institutional investors won't.

Angel Networks are groups of individual investors who pool capital and expertise to collectively fund startups. Problems like discovery gaps (founders can't find angels) and information asymmetry (angels lack deep market knowledge) led to the formation of these networks. Examples: Indian Angel Network, BITS Spark.

Venture Capital (VC) & Private Equity (PE)

VCs are professional investment firms that raise money from wealthy investors and deploy it into high-growth startups. They are equity investors — they buy ownership stakes and expect large returns.

Determinants for VC Investment:

  • Capital Multiplication — Can ₹10 crore become ₹100+ crore?
  • Hyper Growth Trajectory — Is growth exponential, not linear?
  • Equity-Driven Wealth — Returns come from share price appreciation, not dividends
  • Market Dominance & Retention — Can the company become a market leader?

Term Sheet

A term sheet is a preliminary, non-binding document that outlines the key commercial terms of a proposed investment. Receiving a term sheet is a signal that a VC is interested in investing in your startup. It is NOT money — it is intent to invest under specified conditions.

Public Equity Market

The stock exchange where shares of publicly listed companies are bought and sold. Access is highly restricted and regulated because the general public is investing their savings — they need protection from fraud and misinformation.

Secondary Purchases (Secondaries)

A secondary transaction is when a later-stage investor buys existing shares directly from early-stage investors or employees — rather than the company issuing new shares. This happens when the company hasn't yet gone public (IPO) but existing investors want to exit and new investors want to enter.

Startup Valuation and Investment Decision

How do you put a number on a company that has no revenue, no history, and maybe just an idea? This module teaches you the logic and mechanics of startup valuation.

2.1 What Investors Look For in Growth & Expansion Stage

When a startup is past the early stage and seeking growth capital, investors evaluate it differently:

  • Market Opportunity: Is the target market large enough to justify the investment?
  • Analysis of Competition: Who are the competitors? Is the moat (competitive advantage) defensible?
  • Business Model: How does the company make money? Is it sustainable?
  • Existing Investor Willingness: Are current investors ready to invest more? (This is a signal of confidence)
  • Organization and Staffing Plan: Does the company have (or plan to have) the right team?

Early-Stage vs. Evolved-Stage Valuation

Valuation BasisDriven by investor preferences, market conditions, capital availabilityBased on comparable companies, historical financials, forecasts

2.2 Accounting Measures of Valuation

These are ratios used to value companies, especially mature or public companies. For early-stage startups they're used as a reference point.

Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

P/E Ratio = Market Price of Share ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)
  • Market Price: Current trading price of one share on the stock market
  • EPS (Earnings Per Share): Profit After Tax ÷ Number of Equity Shares

The P/E ratio tells you: "How many years' worth of earnings am I paying for this share today?"

If a company earns ₹10 per share and its stock trades at ₹200, the P/E = 20. You're paying 20 times earnings. High P/E = investors expect high future growth. Low P/E = either slow growth or undervalued.

Companies in the same industry tend to have similar P/E ratios because they face similar market conditions, capital structures, and profitability patterns. Companies with higher profitability and growth potential tend to have higher P/E and P/B ratios.

Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio

P/B Ratio = (Value of Assets − Value of Liabilities) ÷ Number of Shares Outstanding
  • This is essentially Net Worth (Equity + Reserves) divided by the number of shares
  • P/B < 1 means the market values the company below its book value (potentially undervalued or distressed)
  • P/B > 1 means the market sees more value than just the physical assets (brand, IP, growth potential)

EV/EBITDA Ratio

EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortization
  • Enterprise Value (EV): Total value of all the company's assets (debt + equity). EV − External Liabilities = Equity Value
  • EBITDA: Operating profit before non-cash items (depreciation, amortization) and financial costs (interest, tax)
  • Why EV/EBITDA is preferred over P/E: Some companies have very different borrowing policies or asset intensities. EV/EBITDA is unaffected by capital structure and accounting differences — it's a "cleaner" comparison
EV/EBITDA is a very common exam question. Remember: it's preferred over P/E because it removes the distortions caused by different debt levels, tax situations, and accounting policies between companies.

2.3 Dilution — The Most Misunderstood Concept

Dilution is the reduction in the percentage ownership of existing shareholders when a company issues new equity (shares).
You own 50% of a 4-slice pizza. If the pizza is made bigger (8 slices), and you don't buy new slices, you now own 25% (2 slices out of 8). Your percentage went down (diluted), but the total pizza is bigger — your 2 slices might still be more valuable if the pizza became more valuable overall.

Dilution is not inherently negative. While your percentage ownership decreases (pro-rata dilution), the monetary value of your holding may increase significantly if the company grows. A 5% stake in a ₹1,000 crore company is worth more than a 20% stake in a ₹50 crore company.

Critical Error: Failing to account for subsequent rounds of financing. If a company needs more cash before an exit occurs, the initial investor's stake will be diluted further. Early investors must plan for this.

2.4 Pre-Money and Post-Money Valuation

You have an empty wallet and I give you ₹100. Before I gave you the money, your wallet was worth ₹0 (pre-money). After I gave you the money, your wallet is worth ₹100 (post-money). The ₹100 I added is the "investment."
Post-Money Valuation = Cash Sought at Funding Round ÷ Percentage of Equity Sought by Investor
  • Post-money valuation = The total value of the company AFTER the investment comes in (includes the new cash)
  • This is what people commonly refer to when they say "Startup X was valued at ₹500 crore"
Pre-Money Valuation = Post-Money Valuation − Cash Sought at Funding Round
  • Pre-money = The value the investor attributes to the existing company before the investment
  • It reflects what the investor thinks the company's assets and capabilities are worth on their own
Example: An investor wants to invest ₹10 crore for 20% equity.
Post-Money Valuation = ₹10 crore ÷ 20% = ₹50 crore
Pre-Money Valuation = ₹50 crore − ₹10 crore = ₹40 crore
Meaning: The investor values the existing company at ₹40 crore. After adding her ₹10 crore, the company is worth ₹50 crore.

Parameters for Valuation

  • Mature companies focus on price per share as the valuation metric
  • Early-stage ventures prioritize percentage of equity granted for a certain amount of cash — because there's no meaningful market price yet

Steps in Valuation of a Startup

  1. Estimate the company's future value at time of exit (e.g., what will it be worth in 5 years?)
  2. Determine the investor's expected return (e.g., 10x return)
  3. Work backwards to determine today's required ownership percentage
  4. Determine how much equity to issue for the investment amount
  5. Account for future dilution from subsequent rounds

Financial Modelling

Financial modelling is the art of translating your business plan into numbers. This module covers what experienced investors actually look for, the red flags in pitch decks, and how to build a financial plan for a startup.

3.1 What Experienced Investors Actually Look For

From interview insights with VCs Parag Dhol and Naganand Doreswamy:

  • Large Market: Is the target opportunity substantial enough to build a large company? Investors need to know there's a big enough prize at the end.
  • Great Team: Chemistry, ambition, and complementary skills. The ideal team structure is "first among equals" — collaborative, not hierarchical with a dominant boss.
  • Competitive Advantage (Moat): What prevents competitors from copying you and winning? Technology, network effects, data, regulatory licenses, brand loyalty.

3.2 Pitch Deck Red Flags

Experienced investors see thousands of pitches. Here's what immediately turns them off:

Red FlagWhat It Signals to the Investor
Complexity and Jargon (violates the "Feynman Rule")If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it yourself. Richard Feynman: "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough."
Unrealistic Projections ("₹100 crore in 5 years" from zero revenue)Shows lack of business sense and understanding of market realities. Called "out of whack" projections.
Lack of Focus (pitching too many things at once)Suggests the founder hasn't deeply thought about which problem is the core one worth solving
Dishonesty ("I have no competition" / false urgency)Every business has competition. Claiming otherwise shows naivety. False urgency ("I need a term sheet in 2 days") destroys trust immediately.
The most critical quality in a post-investment relationship: Intellectual Honesty. Investors want "bad news before good news." Hiding problems destroys trust; proactively sharing bad news builds it.

Post-Investment Relationship

  • Formal: Monthly MIS (Management Information System) reports covering product, sales, and runway; business update meetings
  • Informal: Regular calls and mentoring — engagement should move to higher strategic level and decrease in frequency as the company matures

Key Strategic Insights on VC Funding

  • VC Funding is Strategic, Not Mandatory: Institutional capital is a tool for accelerated growth, not a badge of honor. It comes with governance requirements and loss of absolute freedom.
  • Product-Market Fit (PMF) as a Trigger: The ideal time to raise capital is when your offering begins to resonate insistently with the market — the "Amazon bell" moment (when things just start working).
  • Capital Adequacy Over Dilution: Founders should prioritize raising enough capital to reach the next milestone rather than minimizing dilution. Running out of cash is a terminal (fatal) risk.
"Scrounging" to save equity (raising too little money to avoid dilution) often leads to failure. Cash runs out before you hit the next milestone, and you end up raising a distressed round at a low valuation — causing MORE dilution than if you'd raised properly the first time.

3.4 Cash Budget

A cash budget is a statement of receipts (money coming in) and payments (money going out) over a period of time. It focuses on determining the closing cash balance — indicating whether there will be a positive surplus or a deficit. Note: Cash ≠ Profit. A company can be profitable on paper but have no cash.
Funding Requirement = Peak Operational Cash Deficit + Infrastructure Investment + Technology Investment
  • Operational Cash Deficit: The negative difference between a business's operational receipts and operational payments (you're spending more than you're earning)
  • Infrastructure Investment: Physical assets needed (office, equipment, facilities)
  • Technology Investment: Software, platforms, digital infrastructure

Key insight: "The larger the amount you raise upfront (in first round), the greater the dilution." It is advised to distribute investment in different rounds over time, assigning the right value to investment according to the company's track record at each stage.

Core Assumptions in Financial Modelling

  • Uniform Center Economics: Revenue and cost per unit location doesn't change regardless of growth speed (simplified assumption for modelling)
  • Geographic Cost Consistency: Costs of land, labour, etc. are the same across all geographic locations (again, a modelling simplification)
  • Static Technology Impact: Technology's efficiency contribution doesn't change over time
These assumptions are simplifications for modelling purposes. In reality, costs vary by city, technology improves, and center economics change as you scale.

Investment Deal Structuring

A term sheet isn't just about valuation. It's a complex web of rights, protections, and incentives. This module teaches you how deals are actually structured — and what every clause means.

4.1 What is Deal Structuring?

Structuring is designing a complete financial package — including all terms and conditions (financial AND non-financial) — with the goal of: (1) realizing the investor's return objective, (2) mitigating risks, and (3) aligning the incentives of all stakeholders.

Goals of Founders vs. Investors

PartyPrimary Goals
Investor GoalsRate of return on investment; Liquidity (ability to exit and convert to cash); Contractual control over key decisions
Founder GoalsControl over the enterprise (management freedom); Wealth creation (maximize their own equity value)

Rich vs. King Preference

Research shows founders fall into two psychological archetypes when it comes to equity vs. control:

PreferenceWhat They PrioritizeTrade-off They Accept
💰 Rich (Wealth Maximizers)Maximum financial wealth creationWilling to give up control/equity to bring in resources that increase total company value
👑 King (Control Maximizers)Maximum control and decision-making powerWilling to forgo potential financial gain to maintain authority
Mark Zuckerberg (King preference): Despite taking massive outside investment, he structured Facebook so he retained voting control through dual-class shares. Bill Gates (Rich preference): Gave up large equity stakes early but maximized total wealth by allowing Microsoft to scale faster.

Factors Affecting Deal Structuring

  1. Taxation Efficiency: The structure must be tax-efficient for the company, founder, AND investor. Inefficient structures increase the cost of fundraising.
    Angel Tax (now repealed): Any premium paid for shares above a certain amount was considered taxable income for the receiving company — making fundraising prohibitively expensive.
  2. Preservation of Future Fundraising Potential: Terms that satisfy current investors but alienate future ones are a common pitfall. Must maintain "room" for subsequent rounds.
  3. Incentivizing Key Employees: Must include provisions like ESOP to attract and retain talent.
  4. Regulatory Considerations: Must comply with Companies Act, FEMA, SEBI, and other applicable laws.

4.2 Structuring Alternatives

Warrants

A warrant is a mechanism that allows the warrant holder to buy additional shares in the company at a predetermined price. They are used to incentivize performance — if the company performs better than expected, the founder benefits; if performance is lower than expected, the investor benefits.

Convertible Instruments

A convertible instrument is a non-equity investment (debt) which is intended to eventually convert into equity. It links the conversion price to the performance of the company.

Why use convertible instruments?

  • Reduces Price Risk — If the company performs poorly, the investor gets more shares (better ratio); if it performs well, the founder benefits
  • Reduces Liquidity Risk — Unlike equity, convertible instruments can be redeemed using the company's cash flow if an equity exit isn't possible

Forms: Preference shares, loans, debentures. Conversion can happen at a pre-agreed price or pre-agreed formula.

Conversion Options

TypeWho DecidesWhen Used
Optionally ConvertibleThe investor chooses: convert to equity OR demand repaymentWhen there's uncertainty about whether equity exit is possible
Compulsory ConvertibleMust convert to equity regardless of price or circumstancesWhen both parties are highly confident in the company's valuation trajectory

Cumulative Preference Shares

In cumulative preference shares, the right to a dividend carries forward if the company cannot pay in a given year. If dividends are skipped in Years 1 and 2, all three years of dividends must be paid in Year 3 (if cash is available). The dividend "accumulates" rather than being lost.

4.3 Term Sheets — Deep Dive

A term sheet is a non-binding offer that is contractually unenforceable (except for a few specific clauses like confidentiality and exclusivity). It exists to maintain flexibility for negotiation while capturing the key commercial terms of the proposed deal.

Governance and Board Rights

Most startups are governed by a Board of Directors. Early-stage investors typically nominate a seat on the board. There can also be Independent Members (not representing founders or investors) who provide unbiased decisions.

Minority Protection / Veto Rights (Affirmative Covenants)

If you are a minority shareholder (own less than 50%), your ability to influence outcomes through voting is limited. Veto rights allow minority investors to block specific important decisions. This is why discussing these terms before finalizing the deal is critical.

4.4 Exit-Related Terms

Exit can be natural progression (company matures to IPO) or event-driven (acquisition offer). Exit methods: Acquisition, IPO, Buyback.

Types of Acquirers

  • Strategic Acquirer: Wants to own >50% to get majority voting rights and run the company on their terms. Motivated by strategic value (technology, customers, market position).
  • Secondary/Financial Acquirer: Purely financial motivation — they are observers who want returns on investment. Usually minority shareholders.

Drag Along Clause

If after a certain number of years the founder fails to provide the investor with an appropriate exit, the investor has the right to find an acquirer. If the acquirer needs to buy more shares than just the investor's stake (e.g., needs 80% but investor only has 30%), the investor can drag the founder into selling their shares too.

Tag Along Clause

If the founder (who has 60%) finds a buyer for their shares, and the investor has a Tag-Along clause, the investor can also sell their shares (40%) to the same buyer, on the same terms — even though the buyer didn't specifically want to buy from the investor.

Put and Call Options

  • Put Option: Allows the investor to force the founder to buy back their shares at a predetermined price — providing an exit for the investor
  • Call Option: Allows the investor (or founder) to purchase more shares at a predetermined price — increasing their stake

Liquidation Preference

Liquidation Preference refers to the priority an investor has in receiving payment when the company is liquidated (sold or wound up). The investor can specify they should receive their money FIRST — before founders get anything from the proceeds of a sale.
  • Participation Right: The investor receives their investment back FIRST, then ALSO participates in remaining proceeds proportionally. Can be capped (e.g., 2x or 3x the original investment).
  • Non-Participating: Investor gets their preference amount (e.g., 1x investment) but does not share in remaining proceeds

4.5 Anti-Dilution Protection

Down Round: A funding round where the company raises money at a lower valuation than a previous round. This dilutes earlier investors more than expected.

Series A investor invests at ₹100 crore valuation.
Company struggles, raises Series B at ₹75 crore valuation.
This is a DOWN ROUND. Series A investor's shares are now worth less than she paid.
Anti-dilution protection compensates her for this.

Cash Budgeting & Startup Exit Routes

How do startups plan their cash? And when it's time for investors to get their money back, what are the options? This module covers both the financial planning foundation and the endgame of startup investing.

5.1 Startup Cash Budgeting — Assumptions & Strategic Planning

A cash budget is simply a plan showing how much cash will come in and how much will go out every month. It is NOT profit — it is actual cash. Many startups show profit on paper but run out of actual cash.

Common (Wrong) Assumptions and Their Actual Impact

"Technology will automatically save money"Only if implemented well. Bad tech costs MORE money.
"Customer fees will never change"Competition will force price changes, or you'll need to raise prices as costs rise.

The Connected Chain: Growth → Capital → Dilution

These three are inseparably linked:

A coffee shop startup wants to open 10 outlets in Year 1 instead of 3. They must pay rent and salaries for all 10 outlets months BEFORE any outlet makes money. They need much more cash upfront → must raise more money → must give up more equity.

5.2 Defining and Navigating Investment Exit Routes

An exit is when an investor (or founder) sells their shares in the company and converts their investment back to cash — hopefully at a profit.

  1. Investors need their money back — VCs invest other people's money (pension funds, family offices) and must show returns
  2. Founders also need exits — all their wealth is locked in one illiquid company
  3. Exit sets a real value — when someone buys shares at a price, that becomes the verified market value
  4. Without exit options, investors won't invest — no exit potential = no investment market
  5. Timing conflicts — investor may need to exit (to raise a new fund) while founder wants to wait for higher value

5.3 Fundamentals of an IPO

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company sells its shares to the general public on a stock exchange for the first time. The company becomes "listed." Common people can now buy its shares.

Why IPO Gives Highest Returns

In an IPO, thousands (sometimes tens of thousands) of investors bid for shares. More demand = higher price. There is no upper ceiling on how high demand can push the price.

Zomato IPO: Before IPO, early VC investors held shares valued internally. After IPO, millions of retail investors wanted those shares. Price discovery through mass participation.

Why NOT Every Company Can Do an IPO

  1. Investor Preference Changes: Public market investors have favorites that shift over decades. A cotton yarn company would struggle to find buyers today.
  2. Regulatory Criteria: SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) has stringent eligibility rules. Not every company qualifies.

Direct Costs: Travel, promotional materials, broker commissions, merchant banker fees, transfer agent fees = 7–10% of money raised

After the IPO — Obligations

  1. Continuous Compliance: Listed companies must follow board governance rules and make regular disclosures to stock exchanges. Non-compliance can lead to delisting.
  2. Loss of Privacy: Private companies can keep secrets. Public companies cannot. Example: A listed pharma company must update the stock exchange on clinical trial results — competitors can use this information.

5.4 Trade Sales and Secondary Purchases

A trade sale is selling the company (or part of it) to another company — NOT a financial investor. The buyer is purchasing for strategic reasons: access to technology, products, customers, or talent.
Cisco constantly buys startups to add their products to Cisco's portfolio. They don't want to wait for the startup to grow — they want the technology NOW.

5.5 Buybacks and Self-Liquidating Instruments

Buybacks

A buyback is when the company or founders buy back shares from the investor. Two types:

  • Company Buyback: Company uses its own cash. If company buys back → shares are legally cancelled (extinguished). Legal limit: maximum 25% of share capital under Sections 68 and 69 of Companies Act.
  • Founder Buyback: Founder uses personal money. Founder becomes the new shareholder instead of the investor.
Because of the 25% legal limit, a company buyback cannot always provide a complete exit for an investor who holds more than 25%.

Self-Liquidating Instruments

Instruments that pay themselves back from the company's own cash flow over time — used in countries where IPOs, trade sales, and secondary sales are not viable options (underdeveloped financial markets).

How it works: Company can't do IPO → No buyers for trade sale → But business IS profitable → Investor gives money as preference shares or convertible loan → Company pays back 3-4x the money from profits over 5 years → Investor exits without any share sale

5.6 Strategic Perspectives on Startup Fundraising

  • Thought 1 — Equity is Ideal, But Not the Only Way: If you're not sure about IPO or trade sale, consider quasi-equity instruments (preference shares) that can be repaid from cash flow without needing an exit event.
  • Thought 2 — Early-Stage Financing is a Private Market: Valuations, deal terms, and non-valuation terms are highly confidential. Networking is essential — press reports come with a lag.
  • Thought 3 — Fundraising is Important, But Not the Only Important Thing: Right amount + right time + right terms + right valuation = huge difference. Some founders become obsessed with fundraising and neglect their actual business.
  • Thought 4 — You Will Not Always Get the Best Deal: Private markets are opaque. You may accept harsher terms than a comparable startup. This is normal.

6.1 Funding Goals and Capital Planning

Short-Term Capital Needs (Next 6 Months)

  1. Build a detailed P&L: Revenue by source; Costs (materials, team, marketing, admin, one-time); Include interest, depreciation, taxes

Market Sizing: TAM, SAM, SOM

TAM (Total Addressable Market): The entire global/national market for your product — if you had 100% market share.
SAM (Serviceable Available Market): The portion of TAM you can realistically reach given your geography, distribution, and business model.
SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market): The realistic slice of SAM you can capture in the near term.
Tea startup: TAM = all tea drinkers in India (₹50,000 crore market). SAM = tea drinkers in your target cities who buy premium tea (₹5,000 crore). SOM = what you can realistically capture in Year 1-3 (₹50 crore).

6.3 M&A Valuation and Deal Strategy

M&A (Mergers & Acquisitions) means one company buying or merging with another. A strategic partner is another company in a similar or related business — not just a financial investor.

The 3 Parts of a Strategic Deal

  1. Physics (Business Fit): Demonstrate concrete synergies — what value is created by combining the two companies that neither could create alone?
  2. Chemistry (Cultural Fit): Will the teams work together? Are the founders open to the partner's ideas? Ego clashes destroy deals.
  3. Maths (Valuation): The financial logic — build vs. buy analysis, relative valuation, payback period.

Build vs. Buy Analysis

BuildBuy
Takes 3–5 yearsGet it immediately
Uncertain outcomeAlready proven
Ties up management timeReady to scale
Lower upfront costHigher upfront cost

Your job as a startup founder: make the "buy" case so compelling that buying clearly beats building. Show time saved, cost saved, and competitive head start.

Valuation Arbitrage in M&A

Buyer's stock trades at 8x revenue. Your company is valued at 5x revenue.
Buyer thinks: "I'm buying at 5x but I'm worth 8x — I'm getting a discount!"
Strategy: Target buyers who are valued at HIGH multiples — they feel they're getting a bargain.

How to Pitch for Maximum Valuation in M&A

  1. Create Scarcity (FOMO): Show unique technology, customers, or team. Suggest competitors are also interested.
  2. Show Growth + Synergies: Standalone growth + extra growth from combining = bigger prize.
  3. Create Competition: Approach multiple strategic buyers simultaneously. Let each know others are interested. Run a bidding process. This pushes valuation to the upper range.

  • Stage focus: Do they invest at your company's stage?

Step 3: Prioritize Your Cap Table

  2. Appoint a Banker: A professional intermediary who adds value, structures the narrative, and earns based on outcomes — medium-high success rate

  • Never Burn Bridges: Even if an investor says no, end the conversation politely. Ask for feedback. Keep the door open. The startup ecosystem is small.

  • Keep Building Your Database: Maintain a live database of investor contacts, interactions, and outcomes.

6.6 From Term Sheet to Deal Closure

Binding Clauses in a Non-Binding Term Sheet:

  • Costs: Who pays for legal and diligence costs

Three Types of Due Diligence

A. Commercial Due Diligence

Verify: Market size (visit retail stores, call customers, hire agencies like Nielsen); competition; customer sustainability; revenue quality.

Company must: Set expectations upfront; prep customers before investor calls; maintain confidentiality with consistent narrative.

B. Financial Due Diligence

Verify: Bank statements vs. claimed revenue; quality of earnings (normalized P&L, remove one-time items); balance sheet items (hidden liabilities).

Company must: Create a data room (trackable online repository); assign one finance contact; reconcile all mismatches BEFORE investor asks.

C. Legal Due Diligence

Verify: Contracts (restrictive covenants, approval clauses); regulatory compliance (licenses, PF, ESI, factory compliance); environmental.

Company must: Fix critical issues BEFORE investor sees them; push smaller fixes to "post-deal" timeline.

Legal Foundations of Startup Incorporation & Compliance

Before you can raise money, you need to legally exist. This module teaches you exactly how to incorporate a startup in India, what registrations you need, and how funding works from a legal compliance perspective.

7.1 Business Incorporation and Legal Setup

Incorporation is the legal process of transforming a business into a distinct legal entity — separate from its owners. Once incorporated, the company can own property, sign contracts, hire employees, and be sued — all independent of its founders.

Partnership Firm (Under Indian Partnership Act)

  • Minimum two members (individuals or corporates)
  • Formed by entering into a partnership deed — containing: name, place of business, objectives, capital contribution, profit sharing ratio, dispute resolution clause
  • Registration with Registrar of Firms (state level): Optional (except when immovable property is involved — then mandatory)
  • Disadvantage of unregistered partnership: Cannot sue third parties in case of disputes; cannot apply for startup registration with DPIIT

7.2 LLP vs. Private Limited Company

LLP (Limited Liability Partnership) — Key Features

A hybrid combining features of a partnership firm and a company.

  • Limited Liability: Partners' liability is limited to the capital they have contributed
  • Separate legal entity — LLP can own property, sue or be sued
  • Minimum 2 partners; minimum 2 designated partners (executive role)

Incorporation Process for LLP (via MCA)

  1. Step 1 — Name Approval: File Form RUN-LLP. Two names can be applied simultaneously; one approved if it doesn't resemble existing LLP or trademark.
  2. Step 2 — Incorporation Filing (FiLLiP form): Must be filed within 90 days of name approval. Documents: ID/address proof of partners, designated partner details, registered office proof, subscription document, certification from CA/CS/advocate.
  3. Step 3 — Certificate of Incorporation: Issued by MCA upon processing.
  4. Step 4 — LLP Agreement: Must be filed in Form 3 within 30 days of incorporation.

Private Limited Company — Incorporation Process

Prerequisites
  • Digital Signature Certificate (DSC) — for all directors and shareholders filing forms online
  • Director Identification Number (DIN) — 8-digit unique number; renewed every 3 years by June 30th
  • Identified Registered Office
  • Charter Documents: MOA (Memorandum of Association) and AOA (Articles of Association)

Charter Documents Explained

  • Certificate of Incorporation is issued
  • Basic registrations obtained automatically: PAN, TAN, ESIC, EPF, bank account opening
  • First board meeting must be held within 30 days of incorporation
  • File Form INC 20A (Commencement of Business) — until this is filed, company CANNOT start business or borrow money
  • Issue share certificates to subscribers within 60 days of incorporation

Registered Office Requirements

  • Must be identified at incorporation or within 30 days
  • Must display: company name (English + local language), address, CIN number, GST number, website (if any), email ID
  • Options: Residential house, apartment (if bylaws permit), coworking space (designated space allowed)
  • Virtual office: Strictly NOT allowed — physical, identifiable space required

7.6 Rights Issue — Process and Compliance

A rights issue is a fundamental governance right available to shareholders under the Companies Act. Existing equity shareholders have the right to maintain their percentage ownership when new shares are issued — they get a proportionate offer to participate.

Rights Issue Process (5 Steps)

  1. Convene Board Meeting: Board approves the issuance and decides how much to raise
  2. Proportionate Offer: Offer made to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holding (30% shareholder gets 30% of the offer)
  3. Offer Period: Minimum 7 days, maximum 30 days. Shareholders can: accept, decline, partly accept, or renounce in someone else's favour. Silence = decline.
  4. Allotment: Law prescribes 60 days from receiving application money to allot shares
  5. Post-Allotment Compliance: File Form PAS-3 with ROC within 30 days; update statutory registers; issue duly stamped share certificates within 2 months

7.7 Private Placement and Preferential Allotment

When existing shareholders cannot contribute more, you raise funds from outsiders or a selected group.

Three Critical Pre-Investment Compliance Steps

  1. Check Authorized Share Capital: If you started with ₹15 lakh authorized capital and now want to raise ₹20 crore, you must increase authorized share capital first.
  2. Open Separate Escrow Account: A separate bank account exclusively for receiving share application money (unique to private placement — not required for rights issue)
  3. Obtain Valuation Report: From a registered valuer under Companies Act. Validity: only 90 days. If non-resident investors are involved, also need a merchant banker report.

Private Placement Process (Strict Sequence)

  1. Board meeting (approve issuance; identify investors; call general meeting)
  2. General Meeting — Special Resolution (75% majority; shareholders have right to know about new investors)
  3. File MGT-14 with ROC within 30 days of passing resolution
  4. Issue Private Placement Offer Letter (PAS-4 format) — by name to each identified investor
  5. Investors remit money to designated escrow account
  6. Board approves allotment within 60 days
  7. File PAS-3 with ROC within 15 days — funds cannot be used until this is filed
  8. Post-funding: update registers, issue stamped share certificates, FEMA compliance (if non-residents)
NEVER let investors transfer money BEFORE the offer letter is issued. Even if the investor is eager, this is non-compliance. Penalty: high penalty for startup and founder; investor faces no consequence. This becomes a red flag in your next funding round.

7.8 Convertible Notes for Startups

A convertible note is a hybrid debt instrument with an option to convert to equity. The company either repays the money or converts it into equity. Introduced in 2016 under the Start-up India initiative. Available ONLY to DPIIT-registered startups.

Key Features of Convertible Notes

  • Tenure: up to 10 years from date of issuance
  • Minimum ticket size from a single investor: ₹25 lakhs
  • Can be raised from non-residents (FEMA provisions exist)
  • Least compliance burden compared to rights issue or private placement
  • No valuation report required (shares not issued at time of borrowing; valuation done at future conversion)
  • No separate bank account required; no placement memorandum

Why Convertible Notes Are Preferred at Early Stage

When you need money urgently and don't want to go through the full compliance journey of private placement. The conversion happens later — at the next qualified funding round — at a pre-agreed discount rate.

7.9 ESOP — Employee Stock Option Plan

An ESOP gives employees the right to purchase a certain number of shares in the company at a future date at a predetermined price. It is a non-cash incentive — instead of paying market salary, you give ownership. Employee works, contributes to growth, and later encashes shares at a (hopefully) higher price.

8.2 Venture Capital — Structure and Economics

Venture Capital is high-risk, high-reward money invested into young, ambitious companies. VCs are equity investors — they buy shares and contribute expertise, networks, and capital. They expect substantial growth and possibility of outsized returns in exchange for the risk.

Structure of a VC Fund in India

VC funds are typically structured as AIFs (Alternative Investment Funds) regulated by SEBI. Most fall under Category I AIF — which focuses on early-stage companies, SMEs, and innovation enterprises.

Key SEBI Rules for VC Funds

  • Minimum committed capital before operating: ₹5 crore
  • Minimum investment from any one investor (LP): ₹5 lakhs
  • At least two-thirds of investible corpus must be in unlisted equity or equity-linked instruments
  • Maximum 25% of fund's corpus in a single company (forces diversification)

VC Fund Economics

  • Management Fee: Annual fee paid by LPs to run the fund (covers staff salaries, due diligence costs, admin)
  • Carried Interest (Carry): ~20% of profits when the fund exits its investments. This is the VC's main incentive — they only make serious money if their portfolio companies succeed.

The Funding Ladder

Standard Clauses in These Agreements

  • Representations and Warranties: Your promises about the company. Very detailed. If any representation is false, you may be liable to indemnify the investor. (In India, indemnities ARE enforceable.)
  • Covenants: Forward-looking promises. What the company will do (or not do) after investment. Example: "We will not take new loans above ₹X without investor consent." Breach = investor can sue.
  • Conditions Precedent (CPs): The investor's checklist before money comes in. "Clean your room before you get pocket money."
  • Conditions Subsequent (CSs): Post-closing obligations with specific timelines (e.g., complete within 30 days of closing).

The Closing Process — Step by Step

  1. Investor's lawyer confirms all CPs are completed
  2. Company certifies CPs are complete
  3. Board + shareholder resolutions for share allotment; investor director appointment
  4. Amended AOA adopted at closing
  5. Shares allotted under Section 62 (preferential allotment) or Section 42 (private placement)
  6. Money transferred: Indian investors directly; Foreign investors via Authorized Dealer Bank → FIRC issued → transfer to company account
  7. Filings: PAS-3 (return of allotment); Form FC-GPR (if foreign investor — report to RBI)

8.9 Corporate Governance for Startups

Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. It tells you: who makes which decisions, how information flows, how conflicts are resolved, and how value eventually gets shared.

Key Governance Rights and Mechanisms

Information Rights

When an investor comes in, their information rights are formalized through the SHA and AOA. Common investor information rights: quarterly financial statements, annual audited accounts, management updates, inspection rights (ability to compel document sharing through advisors and conduct audits). Information rights are NOT management rights — investors monitor, not manage.

Board Composition

Board = cockpit of the company. Private companies in India must have at least 2 directors. When investors enter, they typically get a board seat or observer seat.

  • Board Member: Full director — owe fiduciary duty to the COMPANY (not just to the shareholder who nominated them); have voting rights
  • Observer: Can attend and get information; no voting rights — "invited to the party but can't choose the playlist"
Founders must be careful not to give away majority board control early on. Once governance leaves your hands, bringing it back is like trying to put toothpaste back in a tube.
Reserved Matters

Decisions that cannot be taken unless specific shareholders approve. Examples: issuance of new shares; changing board structure; amending AOA/MOA; borrowing above limits; selling substantial assets; entering new business lines; related party transactions.

Reserved matters serve as a "brake system" — founders get day-to-day freedom, investors regulate high-impact decisions. Keep the reserved matters list tight — only company-critical actions that could pivot the company completely.

Economic Rights of Shareholders

  • Pre-emptive Right: Existing shareholders have the right to maintain their proportional ownership when new shares are issued — they must be offered shares pro-rata before new investors come in
  • Anti-Dilution Right: Protection against down rounds. Ensures early investors aren't punished for the company's poor performance when forced to raise at lower valuations.

8.10 Corporate Governance Case Studies

Case 1: Tata vs. Cyrus Mistry

One of India's most dramatic governance battles. Layers of influence: Tata Trusts (two-thirds voting control), independent board, chairman. Cyrus Mistry believed he had authority to steer the enterprise; Tata Trusts believed he was steering it wrong. Mistry's famous statement: "Although I was the pilot, my flight instruments were being controlled by someone else."

Lesson: When governance structures are unclear, even honest business disagreements become existential crises. Even the most iconic institutions need clarity in roles between shareholder influence, board independence, and executive authority.

Case 2: Fortis Healthcare Dispute

Singh brothers diverted funds from listed company to promoter-linked entities. Related party transactions are only allowed when transparent, approved by independent directors, and at arm's-length terms. Fallout: auditors resigned, lenders revolted, SEBI intervened, company became a distressed asset.

Lesson: Board is the custodian of shareholder money. When custodians look the other way, the result is value erosion, loss of credibility, and regulatory consequences.

Case 3: Zee-Sony Merger Collapse

A merger that could have created one of India's largest media giants fell apart because Conditions Precedent became a battleground. Sony believed Zee didn't meet certain CPs (regulatory investigations, leadership disputes). Structure collapsed under the weight of mistrust.

Lesson: Good governance is the foundation of any major deal. If you walk into a negotiation with unresolved investigations, weak controls, or opaque leadership, the other party treats these as deal-killers, not footnotes.

Case 4: Startup World — A Four-Act Play

ActThe ConflictLesson
Act 1: Information RightsInvestors want MIS reports; founders think they're a distractionIf reporting feels like a burden, your systems are already weak. Governance doesn't slow you down — it prevents the car from skidding off the road.
Act 2: Boardroom BattlesFounders bypass board approvals; investors question decisionsOne disagreement in a concentrated shareholder structure can stall the entire business machinery.
Act 3: Anti-Dilution DisputesDown round triggers anti-dilution; investors want full ratchet, founders want weighted averageRead anti-dilution clauses CAREFULLY. Founders often realize too late what "full ratchet" actually means for their ownership.
Act 4: Exit Rights FightsInvestor's fund lifecycle demands exit; founder is emotionally attachedMismatch in exit expectations escalates into disputes. Align on exit timelines before investment.
Overall lesson from all governance disputes: Good governance is not optional. Every major conflict traces back to gaps in fundamental governance — clarity of roles, discipline of board processes, alignment between AOA and SHA, and basic respect for the company as a separate legal person.

Weighted Average Anti-DilutionA formula-based anti-dilution mechanism that proportionally adjusts the conversion price based on the size of the new (lower-priced) round relative to total shares outstanding. Fairer to founders than full ratchet.

The Most Important "One-Liners" for Exam

  • A term sheet is non-binding except for exclusivity, confidentiality, and costs clauses.
  • The Valley of Death is when a startup runs out of money between development and sustainable revenue.
  • Equity is preferred over debt for startups because it is patient, permanent capital that shares both risk and reward.
  • Post-money valuation = Pre-money + Investment.
  • EV/EBITDA is preferred over P/E because it is unaffected by capital structure and accounting differences.
  • AOA supersedes SHA — if SHA provisions aren't incorporated into AOA, they are toothless.
  • Friends and family CANNOT give money to a startup as debt — they can only invest as equity.
  • A down round triggers anti-dilution protections for early investors.
  • In copyright, the creator is the first owner — not the company. IP assignment clauses in employment contracts are non-negotiable.
  • IPO is the most desired exit; buyback is the least preferred.
  • The finance hierarchy for startups: Internal accruals → Debt → Equity (most expensive last).
  • Governance disputes always trace back to gaps in clarity of roles, board processes, and shareholder agreements.
