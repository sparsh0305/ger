NP-01 Pre-Money & Post-Money Valuation (Basic)
A startup raises ₹8 crore from a VC who gets 20% equity in return. Calculate the pre-money and post-money valuation. NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Step 1
Identify what we know: Investment amount = ₹8 crore; Equity given = 20% Post-Money Valuation = Investment ÷ % Equity Sought
Step 2
Calculate Post-Money Valuation: Post-Money = ₹8 crore ÷ 0.20 = ₹40 crore
Step 3
Calculate Pre-Money Valuation: Pre-Money = Post-Money − Investment = ₹40 crore − ₹8 crore = ₹32 crore
✓ Post-Money Valuation = ₹40 crore | Pre-Money Valuation = ₹32 crore

Interpretation: The VC values the existing company (its assets, team, technology) at ₹32 crore BEFORE putting in their money. After their ₹8 crore comes in, the total company is worth ₹40 crore. The VC owns 20% of ₹40 crore = ₹8 crore — exactly what they invested. This confirms the calculation.

NP-02 Pre-Money & Post-Money Valuation (with equity % calculation)
A startup wants to raise ₹15 crore. The lead investor believes the company is worth ₹45 crore right now (before investment). What percentage of equity will the investor receive? What is the post-money valuation? NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Step 1
Identify what we know: Pre-money valuation = ₹45 crore; Investment = ₹15 crore
Step 2
Calculate Post-Money Valuation: Post-Money = Pre-Money + Investment = ₹45 crore + ₹15 crore = ₹60 crore
Step 3
Calculate investor's equity percentage: Equity % = Investment ÷ Post-Money Valuation = ₹15 crore ÷ ₹60 crore = 25%
✓ Post-Money Valuation = ₹60 crore | Investor receives 25% equity
NP-03 Multi-Round Dilution
A startup has the following cap table after founding:
• Founder A: 60%, Founder B: 40%
Series A: Raises ₹10 crore for 25% equity
Series B: Raises ₹30 crore for 20% equity (new round)
Calculate each party's ownership after each round, and the post-money valuations. NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
At Founding
Founder A: 60% | Founder B: 40% | Total: 100%
Series A
Series A investor gets 25% of the NEW post-investment company. Existing holders are diluted proportionally: Remaining % for existing holders = 100% − 25% = 75% Founder A: 60% × 75% = 45% | Founder B: 40% × 75% = 30% Series A investor: 25%
Cap table after Series A: A: 45% | B: 30% | Series A VC: 25%
Post-money valuation: ₹10 crore ÷ 25% = ₹40 crore
Pre-money valuation: ₹40 crore − ₹10 crore = ₹30 crore
Series B
Series B investor gets 20% of the NEW post-B company. Existing holders (A, B, Series A VC) are all diluted proportionally: Remaining % for existing holders = 100% − 20% = 80% Founder A: 45% × 80% = 36% Founder B: 30% × 80% = 24% Series A VC: 25% × 80% = 20% Series B investor: 20%
Post-money valuation: ₹30 crore ÷ 20% = ₹150 crore
After Series B: Founder A: 36% | Founder B: 24% | Series A VC: 20% | Series B VC: 20%
Series A Post-money: ₹40 crore | Series B Post-money: ₹150 crore

Key insight: Founder A went from 60% to 36% — diluted by 24 percentage points across two rounds. But if the company is worth ₹150 crore, Founder A's 36% = ₹54 crore. Far more valuable than 60% of a ₹30 crore pre-money company (= ₹18 crore). Dilution is NOT necessarily bad if company value grows.

NP-04 EPS and P/E Ratio
Company XYZ has: Profit After Tax = ₹50 crore; No. of equity shares = 10 crore; Current market price = ₹80 per share.
Calculate: (a) EPS, (b) P/E Ratio, (c) If a comparable company has P/E of 20, is XYZ overvalued or undervalued? NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): EPS
EPS = Profit After Tax ÷ No. of Equity Shares EPS = ₹50 crore ÷ 10 crore = ₹5 per share
Part (b): P/E Ratio
P/E = Market Price ÷ EPS = ₹80 ÷ ₹5 = 16x
Part (c): Comparison
XYZ's P/E = 16x. Comparable company's P/E = 20x.
XYZ's P/E is LOWER than the comparable → XYZ appears undervalued relative to its peer.
If XYZ reaches the comparable's P/E of 20x: Target price = EPS × P/E = ₹5 × 20 = ₹100 per share.
Potential upside from current ₹80: +₹20 per share (25% upside).
✓ EPS = ₹5 | P/E = 16x | XYZ is undervalued (peers trade at 20x)
NP-05 Book Value, Net Worth, Market Cap
Company ABC has: Share Capital = ₹5 crore; Reserves & Surplus = ₹20 crore; No. of shares outstanding = 5 crore; Current share price = ₹12.
Calculate: (a) Net Worth, (b) Book Value per Share, (c) P/B Ratio, (d) Market Capitalization. NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Net Worth
Net Worth = Share Capital + Reserves & Surplus = ₹5 crore + ₹20 crore = ₹25 crore
Part (b): Book Value/Share
Book Value = Net Worth ÷ No. of Outstanding Shares = ₹25 crore ÷ 5 crore = ₹5 per share
Part (c): P/B Ratio
P/B = Market Price ÷ Book Value = ₹12 ÷ ₹5 = 2.4x This means investors are paying 2.4 times the book value — they believe the company has intangible value (brand, growth potential) beyond its physical assets.
Part (d): Market Cap
Market Cap = Share Price × No. of Shares = ₹12 × 5 crore = ₹60 crore
✓ Net Worth = ₹25 crore | Book Value/Share = ₹5 | P/B = 2.4x | Market Cap = ₹60 crore
NP-06 Funding Requirement Calculation
A startup has modelled a "Cozy" growth path. Key data:
• Maximum operational cash deficit per center: ₹30 lakh/month
• Duration of peak deficit: 6 months
• Maximum number of centers at any time: 5
• Investment (infrastructure) per center: ₹50 lakh
Calculate the total funding requirement. NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Formula
For Lazy/Cozy growth paths: Funding = (Max. Operational Cash Deficit/Center × Max. Centers) + (Investment/Center × Max. Centers)
Part 1: Operational Cash Deficit
Peak deficit per center = ₹30 lakh/month × 6 months = ₹180 lakh per center Total operational deficit = ₹180 lakh × 5 centers = ₹900 lakh = ₹9 crore
Part 2: Infrastructure Investment
Total investment = ₹50 lakh × 5 centers = ₹250 lakh = ₹2.5 crore
Total Funding Requirement
Total = ₹9 crore + ₹2.5 crore = ₹11.5 crore
✓ Total Funding Requirement = ₹11.5 crore
Add a 10–20% contingency buffer on top for unexpected events: ₹11.5 crore × 1.15 = ~₹13.2 crore as a safer ask. Examiners often expect you to mention this.
NP-07 Anti-Dilution — Full Ratchet vs. Weighted Average
Series A investor invested ₹10 crore for 25% equity at a post-money valuation of ₹40 crore (implied share price = ₹40 per share).
The company now raises a down round: ₹5 crore for 25% at a post-money valuation of ₹20 crore (new share price = ₹20 per share).
(a) Explain what happens under Full Ratchet protection.
(b) Which method is fairer to founders? NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Setup
Series A: Paid ₹40/share. New round: ₹20/share. Valuation halved → this is a DOWN ROUND.
Series A's original investment: ₹10 crore. At old price: ₹10 crore ÷ ₹40 = 25 lakh shares.
Full Ratchet
Under full ratchet, Series A's conversion price drops to ₹20 (the new price).
To get the same ₹10 crore "worth" at the new price: New shares entitled = ₹10 crore ÷ ₹20 = 50 lakh shares Series A now holds 50 lakh shares instead of 25 lakh — effectively getting 25 lakh ADDITIONAL shares at no extra cost.
This massively dilutes founders and all other non-protected shareholders.
Weighted Average
The adjustment is proportional to the actual dilution — not a full reset. The new conversion price sits somewhere between ₹40 and ₹20 depending on the size of the new round relative to total shares.
Result: Series A gets some additional shares (partial compensation), but NOT as many as under full ratchet.
This is fairer to founders because the punishment is proportional.
Full Ratchet: Series A goes from 25L to 50L shares (doubles their holding at founder's expense). Weighted Average: Proportional compensation — fairer to all parties.
NP-08 Liquidation Preference — Participating vs. Non-Participating
A VC invested ₹20 crore for 30% equity (post-money valuation ₹67 crore). The startup is acquired for ₹80 crore. The VC has a 1x non-participating liquidation preference. Founders hold the remaining 70%.
(a) How is ₹80 crore distributed under non-participating preference?
(b) How would it differ under a participating preference? NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Non-Participating
VC takes their 1x preference first: ₹20 crore (their original investment).
Remaining: ₹80 crore − ₹20 crore = ₹60 crore goes to common shareholders (founders). VC gets: ₹20 crore | Founders get: ₹60 crore BUT wait: if VC had no preference and just took their 30% proportionally: ₹80 crore × 30% = ₹24 crore.
Here the VC would choose to take ₹24 crore (proportional) over ₹20 crore (1x preference). So in this case, the VC would CONVERT to equity and take proportional share. VC: ₹24 crore (30%) | Founders: ₹56 crore (70%)
Participating
VC takes 1x preference FIRST: ₹20 crore.
Then VC ALSO participates in remaining ₹60 crore proportionally (30%): VC additional share = ₹60 crore × 30% = ₹18 crore VC total = ₹20 crore + ₹18 crore = ₹38 crore Founders get = ₹60 crore × 70% = ₹42 crore
Non-Participating: VC = ₹24 crore, Founders = ₹56 crore | Participating: VC = ₹38 crore, Founders = ₹42 crore

Lesson: Participating liquidation preference is MUCH more investor-friendly. Founders should resist it in negotiations. The difference here is ₹14 crore — entirely at founders' expense.

NP-09 IPO Cost Calculation
A startup plans to raise ₹500 crore through an IPO. Calculate: (a) Estimated direct cost of the IPO, (b) Net proceeds to the company, (c) If top management spent 10 months on the IPO and the company's monthly revenue is ₹20 crore, estimate the indirect cost (opportunity cost). NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Direct Cost
Direct cost = 7–10% of amount raised Minimum (7%): ₹500 crore × 7% = ₹35 crore Maximum (10%): ₹500 crore × 10% = ₹50 crore Best estimate (midpoint 8.5%): ₹500 crore × 8.5% = ₹42.5 crore
Part (b): Net Proceeds
Net Proceeds = ₹500 crore − ₹42.5 crore = ₹457.5 crore (at 8.5% cost)
Part (c): Indirect Cost (Opportunity Cost)
If management diverted attention for 10 months and assumed 20% revenue loss during that period: Revenue foregone = ₹20 crore × 10 months × 20% = ₹40 crore (approx.) This is an approximation — the point is that indirect costs are real and significant.
✓ Direct cost: ₹35–50 crore | Net proceeds: ~₹457 crore | Significant indirect opportunity cost
NP-10 ESOP Valuation & Grant
A startup grants 10,000 ESOPs to an employee on January 1, 2023. Grant price = ₹10/share. Vesting: 25% per year over 4 years. By January 1, 2026 (3 years later), the fair market value of shares = ₹150/share.
(a) How many options have vested by Jan 1, 2026?
(b) What is the profit per share if the employee exercises on Jan 1, 2026?
(c) What is the total profit on all vested options? NUMERICAL
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Vested Options
Vesting = 25% per year. By Jan 1, 2026 = 3 years have passed. Vested = 10,000 options × 25% × 3 years = 7,500 options (Remaining 2,500 options vest in Year 4)
Part (b): Profit per Share
Profit per share = FMV − Exercise Price = ₹150 − ₹10 = ₹140 per share
Part (c): Total Profit
Total profit = 7,500 shares × ₹140 = ₹10,50,000 (₹10.5 lakh)
✓ 7,500 options vested | ₹140 profit/share | Total profit = ₹10.5 lakh
Note: This ₹10.5 lakh is taxable! The taxation of ESOPs is an important practical consideration. In India, ESOPs are taxed as perquisites at the time of exercise (FMV − exercise price = taxable income), and then again as capital gains when shares are sold.

Module 1 — Foundations

CQ-01 Why is debt financing generally unsuitable for early-stage startups? Give at least 3 specific reasons. EASY
▸ Model Answer
  • Uncertain cash flows: Startups have no predictable revenue yet. Debt requires fixed periodic interest payments regardless of performance — a startup that can't make its payment faces default.
  • No collateral: Banks require physical assets as security. Early-stage startups usually have no land, machinery, or property to pledge.
  • Asymmetric risk: If the startup succeeds, the lender only gets their fixed interest — they don't share in the upside. If it fails, the founder loses personal assets (if personal guarantees were given). This is a bad deal for founders.
  • Consequences of default are severe: The default process — soft reminders → legal notice → court → insolvency — can destroy the business and the founder's personal credit.
  • Equity provides "patient capital": Equity investors understand startup risk and are willing to wait years for returns. Debt providers are not.
CQ-02 Distinguish between authorized share capital, issued capital, subscribed capital, and paid-up capital. Use an analogy to explain. EASY
▸ Model Answer

Analogy: Think of it like a pizza restaurant.

  • Authorized Share Capital: The maximum size pizza the kitchen is licensed to make (set in MOA). You can always bake a smaller pizza, but you can never legally bake bigger than this without getting a new license. Maximum ceiling.
  • Issued Capital: The size of the pizza actually baked today and put on the menu for sale. You officially offered this many slices to the public.
  • Subscribed Capital: How many slices customers actually agreed to buy. The real demand from investors.
  • Paid-Up Capital: How many slices customers have actually paid for. Real money received by the company.

Typical relationship: Authorized ≥ Issued ≥ Subscribed ≥ Paid-Up.

CQ-03 What is the "Valley of Death"? Why do startups fall into it? What types of financing help bridge it? MEDIUM
▸ Model Answer

Definition: The Valley of Death is the critical period in a startup's life when the initial funding has been spent but the company hasn't yet generated sufficient revenue to be self-sustaining. It's the gap between spending and earning.

Why startups fall in:

  • Development takes longer than expected
  • Customer acquisition costs are higher than projected
  • Revenue ramp is slower than planned
  • The initial funding was insufficient for the growth strategy chosen

Financing that bridges it:

  • Angel investors (bridge the pre-seed to seed gap)
  • Accelerator funding (Y Combinator, etc.)
  • Government grants (developmental agencies)
  • Convertible notes (quick, low-compliance debt that converts to equity)
  • Friends and family (as equity investment)

Module 2 — Valuation

CQ-04 Why is EV/EBITDA preferred over the P/E ratio for comparing companies across an industry? Give a concrete example where P/E would mislead. MEDIUM
▸ Model Answer

EV/EBITDA advantages:

  • Capital structure neutral: P/E is affected by how much debt a company has. A heavily indebted company pays more interest, so its PAT (profit after tax) is lower → P/E looks inflated. EV/EBITDA measures value before interest payments.
  • Tax neutral: Companies in different states/countries pay different tax rates, affecting PAT. EBITDA is pre-tax.
  • Accounting neutral: Depreciation policies differ between companies (straight-line vs. reducing balance). EBITDA adds back depreciation.

Example where P/E misleads: Company A (no debt, PAT = ₹100 crore) and Company B (heavy debt, PAT = ₹40 crore — same operating profit but ₹60 crore interest). Both trade at market cap of ₹800 crore. Company A's P/E = 8x; Company B's P/E = 20x. P/E makes Company B look more "expensive" but they have the same operating business — the difference is just their capital structure. EV/EBITDA would show them as equally valued (both have EV/EBITDA ≈ 5x if EBITDA = ₹160 crore).

CQ-05 A founder says: "I don't want to raise any more money because I don't want to dilute my ownership further." As an advisor, what would you tell her? HARD
▸ Model Answer

This is a classic "King preference" mistake. Here's what you tell her:

  • Dilution is NOT the same as losing value. Yes, your percentage goes down. But if the company's total value goes up more than the dilution percentage, your absolute wealth increases. A 30% stake in a ₹500 crore company (= ₹150 crore) is better than a 70% stake in a ₹50 crore company (= ₹35 crore).
  • Running out of cash is a terminal risk. If the company runs out of cash before reaching the next milestone, it dies. That means 0% of ₹0. There is no dilution worse than that.
  • "Scrounging" causes MORE dilution. If you raise too little now and the company struggles, you'll be forced to raise a distressed round at a lower valuation — causing MORE dilution than a clean round today.
  • The right question is: What will this capital be used for? If it will help hit the next milestone (PMF, revenue target) that unlocks a much higher valuation for the following round, the dilution is an investment, not a loss.
  • Rule of thumb: Raise enough to get to the next milestone with a 6-month buffer. Capital adequacy > dilution minimization.

Module 4 — Deal Structuring

CQ-06 Explain the difference between a Drag Along and a Tag Along clause. Which one is more powerful, and for whom? Give a scenario for each. MEDIUM
▸ Model Answer
Tag AlongDrag Along
Who holds itMinority investor (protects them)Majority investor or large shareholder
What it allowsJoin a sale the founder initiatesForce all shareholders to sell when investor wants to exit
More powerful forInvestor (as minority protection)Investor (ability to force exit)

Tag Along Scenario: Founder (60%) finds a buyer who will pay ₹150/share for the whole 60% stake. If investor (40%) has tag along, they can sell their 40% to the same buyer at the same ₹150/share price — even though the buyer only approached the founder.

Drag Along Scenario: Investor wants to sell to a strategic acquirer who needs 80% of the company. Investor has 40%. Investor uses drag along to FORCE the founder (60%) to sell their stake too, enabling the full 100% acquisition even though the founder didn't want to sell yet.

Drag along is more powerful because it removes the founder's control over their own exit timing. Founders should negotiate hard to limit drag along rights (e.g., minimum price floor, minimum years before drag can be triggered).

CQ-07 What are the four factors that affect deal structuring? Explain each with a real-world example. MEDIUM
▸ Model Answer
  1. Taxation Efficiency: The deal structure must be tax-efficient for ALL three parties — company, founder, investor. The now-repealed Angel Tax is the best example: premium paid above market value was treated as company income and taxed at ~30%. This made early-stage fundraising prohibitively expensive. A tax-efficient structure uses instruments (like CCPS, convertible notes) that minimize tax friction.
  2. Preservation of Future Fundraising Potential: Terms that make current investors happy but alienate future investors are dangerous. Example: Giving Investor A a "most favoured nation" clause (ensuring they always get the best terms) may prevent you from offering Investor B necessary rights in the next round, because it would trigger a ratchet for Investor A that makes the company unfundable.
  3. Incentivizing Key Employees (ESOP): A startup's key employees — engineers, sales leads, product managers — could leave for higher salaries at large companies. ESOPs keep them invested in the company's success. Without ESOP provisions in the structure, these employees have no reason to stay.
  4. Regulatory Considerations: Indian startups must comply with Companies Act 2013, FEMA (for foreign investors), and SEBI (for listed companies). A structure that works commercially but violates FEMA — say, giving non-standard exit options to a foreign investor — will be unenforceable and expose founders to penalties.

CQ-09 What is a "warm introduction" and why is it so much more effective than a cold outreach to investors? How would you engineer a warm introduction if you don't know the investor? MEDIUM
▸ Model Answer

What it is: A warm introduction is when a mutual contact (someone the investor already knows and trusts) introduces you to the investor. The investor's trust in the mutual contact transfers partially to you.

Why it's more effective: VCs receive hundreds of pitch decks every week. Cold emails are filtered out by pattern recognition — they look like every other cold email. A warm intro bypasses this filter because: (1) the investor opens it (trust from referrer), (2) they read it with a charitable lens (assumes the referrer pre-screened), (3) they feel social obligation to respond (can't ghost someone their friend introduced).

How to engineer a warm intro without knowing the investor:

  1. Research the investor on LinkedIn and Twitter/X. Understand their thesis and past investments.
  2. Find 2nd-degree connections — other founders they've invested in, ex-colleagues, LinkedIn mutual connections.
  3. Reach out to those founders first: "I'm building X, I saw you work with [Investor]. Would you be willing to give me 15 minutes to see if an intro makes sense?" Most founders pay it forward.
  4. Alternatively: Attend events the investor speaks at. A brief in-person conversation at a conference becomes "we met at TechSparks" — which is far warmer than cold email.
  5. Get covered by press in your domain — investors scan for interesting companies in sectors they follow. Being written about is a passive warm signal.

Modules 7–8 — Legal & Governance

CQ-10 Explain the complete process of issuing ESOPs in a private limited company. What approvals are needed? What are the eligibility restrictions? What is the minimum vesting period? HARD
▸ Model Answer

Process:

  1. Consult with tax and legal advisors to design the ESOP scheme structure
  2. Draft the ESOP Scheme document (not just an employment letter mention)
  3. Obtain Board Approval for the scheme
  4. Obtain Shareholders Approval by Special Resolution (75% majority)
  5. File MGT-14 with ROC (within 30 days of passing the resolution)
  6. Board formally grants options to eligible employees (recording the grant in a grant letter)
  7. After vesting period: employees exercise options (apply + pay exercise price)
  8. Board approves allotment of shares; paid-up capital increases
  9. If non-resident employees: file with RBI

Eligibility:

  • ✓ Permanent employees (India or abroad)
  • ✓ Directors of the company
  • ✓ Employees/directors of holding or subsidiary companies
  • ✗ Promoter group (unless DPIIT-registered startup within 10 years)
  • ✗ Independent directors
  • ✗ Directors holding directly or indirectly ≥10% of outstanding equity (unless DPIIT startup)

Minimum vesting period: 1 year (cliff period) from the grant date. No options can be exercised before this minimum period. Typical vesting: 4 years with 25% per year (25% vests after Year 1, then monthly/quarterly thereafter).

CQ-11 Why does Indian law require that SHA (Shareholders Agreement) provisions be incorporated into the AOA? What happens if they're NOT? Give a real-world implication. HARD
▸ Model Answer

The legal hierarchy: Under Indian company law, the AOA (Articles of Association) is the supreme governing document for the company's internal affairs. Any agreement between shareholders (the SHA) that contradicts or goes beyond the AOA is subordinate to the AOA.

The problem: If an investor negotiates important rights in the SHA — say, veto rights over certain board decisions, tag-along rights, anti-dilution protections — but these are NOT incorporated into the AOA, then under the Companies Act, the AOA governs. The SHA provisions exist "in the air" — they are a contractual agreement between parties, but they don't bind the company itself or third parties.

What this means in practice:

  • The company issues new shares without offering the investor their pre-emptive right (because the AOA doesn't require it)
  • A court may not enforce the SHA provision because the AOA says otherwise
  • A new incoming investor or buyer of shares is not bound by the SHA they weren't party to

Real-world implication: In the Zee-Sony merger case, governance structures weren't clearly documented. When disputes arose, neither party could definitively enforce their position because the contractual terms weren't properly embedded in the constitutional documents. This is why investors make it a Condition Precedent to amend the AOA at deal closing.

Scenario-Based & Case Questions

These are the hardest question types — they require you to apply multiple concepts simultaneously. Practice these thoroughly.

SC-01 The Compliance Disaster
Startup "MediTech" was incorporated in 2021. During a Series A due diligence in 2024, the following is discovered:
• No board meetings have been held since incorporation
• Auditor was never formally appointed
• Two co-founders deposited ₹80 lakh each as loans (no board approval, no DPT-3 filed)
• Share certificates were never issued
• IP (a medical algorithm) was developed by a contractor but no IP assignment agreement was signed

(a) Identify all legal violations. (b) What should be the Conditions Precedent (CPs) in the term sheet? (c) Will the investor likely walk away? CASE
▸ Model Answer

(a) Legal Violations:

  • No board meetings: Companies Act requires at least 4 board meetings per year, not more than 120 days apart. 3 years of non-compliance = ~12 missed meetings. Penalty: per-meeting fines for each director.
  • Auditor not appointed: Must be appointed within 30 days of incorporation. Auditor tenure under Companies Act 2013 is fixed for 5 years — appointing for 1 year as a fix is not possible. Cannot be backdated.
  • Co-founder loans without board approval: All director borrowings require board approval and annual DPT-3 filing. ₹1.6 crore in unrecorded, unapproved loans. This also raises money-laundering compliance concerns (directors must declare money is from own earnings).
  • Share certificates not issued: Must be issued within 60 days of allotment. Delay = penalty on company and directors. Ownership documentation is legally questionable.
  • IP not assigned: The contractor legally owns the medical algorithm. The company has no rights to its own core product. This is existential.

(b) Conditions Precedent:

  1. File for adjudication/compounding for all ROC non-compliances (board meetings, auditor, DPT-3 — expect 6-9 months and significant fines)
  2. Convene a board meeting to ratify all past decisions (loan approvals, share allotments)
  3. File all overdue DPT-3 forms reporting director loans
  4. Issue share certificates (with stamp duty, which cannot be backdated — pay penalty for delay)
  5. Execute IP Assignment Agreement with the contractor for the algorithm (may require negotiation and payment)
  6. File provisional patent application to protect the algorithm going forward
  7. Appoint auditor formally with proper board resolution

(c) Will investor walk away? Possibly, if: IP assignment cannot be obtained from the contractor (he may refuse or demand unreasonable payment); adjudication process drags beyond 12 months; investor's fund timeline can't accommodate the delay. More likely: investor issues term sheet with extensive CPs, reduced valuation to price the compliance risk, and indemnities protecting them from liability arising from pre-investment violations. The case study from the module (GCL Technologies) shows this exact pattern — investment delayed, sometimes denied.

SC-02 The Exit Timing Conflict
A VC fund invested ₹15 crore in "FoodX" (a food-tech startup) in 2019 for 20% equity at a post-money valuation of ₹75 crore. The fund's 7-year lifecycle ends in 2026 and LPs need returns. The market is currently slow for food-tech IPOs. Founder doesn't want to sell — he believes FoodX will be worth ₹500 crore in 3 more years.

The VC has: a drag-along clause (can trigger after Year 6), a right to force IPO registration after Year 7, a liquidation preference of 1.5x.

(a) What options does the VC have? (b) What options does the founder have? (c) What is the VC's minimum acceptable exit price given their 1.5x preference? CASE
▸ Model Answer

(a) VC's Options:

  • Trigger drag-along clause (Year 6+): Find a strategic or financial buyer willing to acquire the company. If buyer offers sufficient price, drag founder to sell their stake. VC doesn't need founder's consent once drag is triggered.
  • Force IPO registration (Year 7+): Company must file for IPO listing. However, with a slow market, IPO may be difficult to price attractively.
  • Secondary sale: Sell VC's 20% stake to another PE/VC fund (secondary transaction). This exits the VC without the company being sold or going public. Company continues as is.
  • Negotiate with founder: Accept a partial exit (founder buys back some shares using company cash — up to 25% of share capital) or founder arranges financing to buy the VC out personally.

(b) Founder's Options:

  • Raise a new round at a higher valuation — this may trigger secondary sale by VC (VC exits to incoming investor)
  • Bring in a strategic investor at a good valuation that gives VC a partial exit
  • Negotiate an extension with the VC (VCs sometimes extend if they believe in the upside — but LPs must agree)
  • Find a different investor to buy out the VC's stake (secondary transaction)
  • Accept that drag-along may be triggered and negotiate the best possible sale terms

(c) VC's Minimum Acceptable Exit Price:

1.5x liquidation preference on ₹15 crore investment = ₹22.5 crore minimum return.
If the sale is participatory: VC gets ₹22.5 crore FIRST + 20% of remaining proceeds.
If non-participatory: VC gets higher of ₹22.5 crore OR 20% of total proceeds.
At 20% of total proceeds: To get ≥₹22.5 crore, total exit value must be ≥₹112.5 crore.
Minimum acceptable total sale price = ~₹112.5 crore (giving VC exactly 20% = ₹22.5 crore)

SC-03 The Growth Strategy Decision
"EduStart" has modelled three growth paths for its tutoring centers:
• Lazy: 3 centers, peak deficit ₹20L/center/month for 4 months, infra ₹40L/center
• Cozy: 8 centers, peak deficit ₹25L/center/month for 5 months, infra ₹50L/center
• Crazy: 20 centers, peak deficit ₹2 crore/month for 8 months, infra ₹60L/center + tech investment ₹5 crore

Calculate funding requirement for each path. Then recommend the most appropriate path for a startup at Series A stage with a VC asking for 3x returns in 5 years. CASE
▸ Step-by-Step Solution
Lazy Path
Operational deficit = ₹20L × 4 months × 3 centers = ₹240L = ₹2.4 crore Infra investment = ₹40L × 3 centers = ₹120L = ₹1.2 crore Total Lazy = ₹2.4 + ₹1.2 = ₹3.6 crore
Cozy Path
Operational deficit = ₹25L × 5 months × 8 centers = ₹1,000L = ₹10 crore Infra investment = ₹50L × 8 centers = ₹400L = ₹4 crore Total Cozy = ₹10 + ₹4 = ₹14 crore
Crazy Path
Operational deficit = ₹2 crore × 8 months = ₹16 crore (total, not per center) Infra investment = ₹60L × 20 centers = ₹1,200L = ₹12 crore Tech investment = ₹5 crore Total Crazy = ₹16 + ₹12 + ₹5 = ₹33 crore
Lazy: ₹3.6 crore | Cozy: ₹14 crore | Crazy: ₹33 crore

Recommendation: For a VC needing 3x returns in 5 years, the Cozy Path is most appropriate at Series A. Here's why: Lazy (₹3.6 crore) is too small to create a company worth raising VC money for — insufficient scale for 3x returns. Crazy (₹33 crore) is too aggressive for Series A — the team and systems aren't proven at scale yet, and the operational risk is enormous. Cozy (₹14 crore) provides meaningful scale (8 centers), tests the model in multiple locations, builds operational muscle, and creates a track record for Series B. It's large enough to generate returns, manageable enough to execute well.

Quick-Fire Definitions (1-2 sentence answers)

V-01 What is patient capital? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

Patient capital refers to equity investment where the investor is willing to wait for a long period — often 5-10 years — without expecting regular returns, understanding that the startup needs time to grow and create value before an exit becomes possible.

V-02 What is the difference between a trade sale and a secondary sale? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

A trade sale is when shares are sold to another company (strategic buyer) who wants the startup for strategic reasons — technology, customers, market access. A secondary sale is when shares are sold to another financial investor (another VC/PE fund) who wants the investment purely for financial returns. In a trade sale, the buyer is a company; in a secondary, the buyer is a fund.

V-03 What is a "down round"? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

A down round is when a startup raises a new funding round at a lower valuation than the previous round — meaning the company's worth has decreased in the eyes of the market. It dilutes existing investors more than expected and triggers anti-dilution protections. Example: raised Series A at ₹100 crore valuation, now raising Series B at ₹75 crore.

V-04 What is carried interest and why does it exist? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

Carried interest (carry) is typically 20% of the profits a VC fund generates for its LPs. It exists to align the fund manager's incentives with investors — fund managers only earn significant money if their portfolio companies succeed and are exited at good returns. It converts fund managers from salaried employees into performance-driven partners.

V-05 What is the Feynman Rule and how does it apply to startup pitching? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

The Feynman Rule states: "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." In pitching, if a founder cannot explain their product in clear, jargon-free language, experienced investors interpret this as the founder not fully understanding their own business — which is a major red flag, not just a communication issue.

V-06 What is the difference between an incubator and an accelerator? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

Incubator: A longer-term (1-3 years) program that provides infrastructure, mentorship, and resources to very early-stage startups developing their concept. Focus is on nurturing. Accelerator: An intensive, short-term (3-6 months) program that rapidly scales startups that already have a basic product or traction. Focus is on accelerating growth. Famous accelerators include Y Combinator and 500 Global. Accelerators typically take equity (2-7%); incubators may or may not.

V-07 Why can friends and family NOT give money to a startup as a loan? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

Under the Companies Act 2013, companies can only accept deposits from categories listed as "exempted deposits." Friends and family do not fall under any of these exempt categories — they are neither directors, shareholders, nor banks. Accepting money from them as a loan is treated as an illegal deposit. The alternative: they can invest as equity shareholders by buying shares in the company.

V-08 What is Form FC-GPR and when must it be filed? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

FC-GPR (Foreign Currency — Gross Provisional Return) is a form filed with the Reserve Bank of India reporting details of foreign direct investment received — including how much foreign money was received, at what price, and how many shares were issued to the foreign investor. It must be filed within 30 days of allotting shares to a foreign investor. Most investors insist it's filed on the closing date itself.

V-09 What is a "moat" in startup investing? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

A moat (competitive advantage) is a structural barrier that prevents competitors from easily copying your business and taking your customers. Types include: technology moats (patents, proprietary algorithms), network effect moats (more users = more valuable, like LinkedIn), switching cost moats (too painful to change), cost advantage moats (cheaper to produce), and brand moats (Apple, Nike). A strong moat is one of the most important things VCs evaluate — without it, any business success will quickly be replicated by better-funded competitors.

V-10 What is the difference between MOA and AOA? Which one supersedes the other? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

MOA (Memorandum of Association): The company's external constitution — defines what the company IS and what it can DO (objectives, name, location, authorized capital). Governs relationship with the outside world. AOA (Articles of Association): The company's internal rulebook — defines HOW the company operates (board meetings, share transfers, director appointments). In conflicts between documents: MOA supersedes AOA; AOA supersedes the Shareholders Agreement (SHA). This is why SHA provisions must be incorporated into AOA to be enforceable.

V-11 What is a "virtual office" and why is it not allowed as a registered office for a company? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

A virtual office is a service that provides a business address (often in a prestigious location) without any actual physical workspace. The Companies Act 2013 requires that a registered office be a PHYSICAL, identifiable space capable of receiving all communications at all times — a place where a regulator, creditor, or legal notice can actually reach the company. A virtual office cannot guarantee physical presence, so it is not permitted as a registered office.

V-12 What is "psychic income" in the context of investor motivation? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

Psychic income is the psychological satisfaction and non-financial reward an investor derives from the investing process — such as the excitement of mentoring a young founder, the pride of being associated with a successful company, or the status of being known as an active investor in a hot sector. It explains why many successful entrepreneurs become angel investors even when the financial returns may not justify the risk — they value the intangible experience itself.

V-13 What is "product-market fit" (PMF) and why is it the ideal time to raise VC capital? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

Product-market fit is the point at which your product satisfies the needs of the market so well that demand becomes strong and organic — customers seek you out, retention is high, and word-of-mouth grows. It's the "Amazon bell" moment when something just starts working. It's the ideal time to raise VC because: (a) you can demonstrate the business model works, reducing investor risk; (b) you need capital to SCALE a proven model, not to discover one; (c) your negotiating position is strongest when there's clear evidence of traction.

V-14 What is a "data room" in the context of due diligence? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

A data room is a secure, virtual (online) repository where the startup organizes and shares all documents requested during due diligence — financial statements, contracts, incorporation documents, IP filings, board minutes, employment agreements, and regulatory licenses. It should be trackable (you know which documents have been viewed), organized with clear folders, and have controlled access. The key principle: documents cannot be created AT the time of due diligence — they must already exist. Losing timeline during due diligence due to poor document management can cause investors to lose interest.

V-15 Why is the "first among equals" team structure preferred by VCs over a boss-subordinate hierarchy? VIVA
▸ Ideal Answer

VCs prefer "first among equals" because startups require rapid decision-making, creative problem-solving, and diverse expertise. A collaborative team where the CEO is the most respected person (not the most powerful by title) tends to: (a) retain co-founders who might leave if they feel undervalued; (b) generate better decisions through genuine debate rather than rubber-stamping; (c) signal to investors that the team can handle the founder relationship — which typically becomes the #1 source of startup failure at growth stage. Hierarchical teams also tend to be more brittle under stress.

GOVERNANCE PRINCIPLES (10)
  1. Information rights ≠ management rights (monitor, not manage)
  2. Board observer = attends meetings, no vote
  3. Director owes fiduciary duty to COMPANY, not nominating shareholder
  4. Reserved matters = investor "brake system" on big decisions
  5. Participating liquidation preference is more investor-friendly
  6. Pre-emptive rights prevent unwanted dilution in new rounds
  7. Tata-Mistry: clarity of roles between shareholder and board
  8. Fortis: related party transactions need arm's-length approval
  9. Zee-Sony: CPs protect the party with more at risk
  10. Good governance = difference between smooth growth and conflict
